# Anchors and the lineup test

The lineup test is the main countermeasure to a reviewer flattering its own writing, and it only works if the anchors are real. **A lineup run against remembered examples is not a lineup** - it's the same session's taste wearing a procedure.

## Building the anchor set

Keep, in a file the review can actually open:

- **Three to five approved pieces**, each with **the one sentence it argues** written beside it. The sentence makes the anchor teachable; without it, the anchor only demonstrates a surface.
- **Three to five rejected pieces, with the verdict that killed them**, in the words they were killed with. Blunt verdicts teach more than diplomatic ones, because they name the thing.
- **Praised fragments** - single lines called out as good, even inside pieces that were rejected. They prove the *moves* were never the problem, only their mechanical use. On one project a line was praised warmly inside a piece the same reviewer called generic two paragraphs later.

**Calibrate against the owner's own approved writing, not against professional work in the genre.** This is the most common way an anchor set goes wrong. Importing the gestures of a form you aren't writing - the machinery of long-form investigation in a 120-word piece that did no investigation - produces exactly the imitation this skill set exists to catch. A wider craft reference is a menu of ideas, never a specification.

## Running the test

Mix the candidate, unlabelled, with two approved anchors and two rejected ones. Then:

1. Rank all five by **"written by someone with a point of view"**.
2. **Name the templated one.**

Ranking resists flattery in a way absolute grading doesn't, and question 2 does the work: asked to *find* the templated item, a reader will find one - and if it's your candidate, you have your answer.

**A method that picks out the dud among your own anchors is worth adopting.** One project validated its ending method this way: it ran each anchor's last sentence backwards to the feeling it answered, and the method independently identified the ending the reviewer had already called weak.

## Maintaining the set

**Anchors go stale silently, and nothing checks them.** A rule gets re-read; an example gets absorbed. On one project a rule banning a class of wording landed and held, while the anchors underneath kept quoting the banned wording for a full day afterwards, teaching the opposite of the rule to everyone who read them. **When a rule change alters shipped text, re-read the anchors in the same pass.**

- **Quote what actually shipped, not a draft.** Anchors that quote unpublished alternates calibrate everyone against text the product doesn't contain.
- **When an anchor breaks a later rule, don't silently delete it.** Keep it, marked, with a note on what is no longer allowed and why the move it demonstrates is still available. Deleting it loses the lesson and invites someone to rediscover the line.
