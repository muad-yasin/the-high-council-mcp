# Good-News Writing - the warm register

Writing about someone real doing something good is its own craft problem, distinct from writing about wrongdoing. The failure mode is not dishonesty - it's usually that positive copy either curdles into a press release, or reaches sideways for a villain that isn't in the story, or backhands the subject with praise that imports the very doubt it claims to dismiss. This skill is the register that avoids all three.

**Everything in sower-writer's guardrail binds here unchanged - the never-invent-a-quote rule, name transformation, the fact line, source grading.** This file does not restate that; read it there (`sower-writer:fact-checking`). The hard rules below are *additional*, register-specific, and mostly stricter than the general case, because the subject here is real, alive, and being written about warmly rather than adversarially - which raises the bar on every claim, not lowers it.

## When this skill, and when not

Run this when **the piece's engine is someone's good choice and there is no wrongdoing in the verified facts.** A founder who did right by customers, a company that refused a profitable-but-ugly shortcut, a public figure's genuine act of generosity, an organization whose only unusual feature is that it does the obviously right thing nobody else bothers with.

- **A real contrast in the record, not a manufactured one.** The test: does the contrast with how the industry normally behaves exist in the verified facts, or would you have to build it? If you'd have to build it, there's no story - underselling a real contrast is recoverable; inventing a sideways jab at "everyone else" is not. When in doubt, write it warm and small rather than reaching.
- **Memorial or tribute content is not this skill's register.** No humor anywhere near a death or a loss; that's a different, quieter register entirely.
- **A subject's own words, in their own voice, are not this skill's to write** - this is coverage *of* someone, never a ghostwritten version of their own voice.
- **Investigative or adversarial coverage of the same subject, even a sympathetic one, is not this skill's job** - if there's a villain in frame (an official, a smearing rival, a system the subject is up against), that's straight drafting/fact-checking, not the warm register.

## Hard rules, additional to the general guardrail

1. **A real quote, verbatim, traced to a primary source - or no quote at all.** There is no generic-unnamed-voice move in this register the way there might be in adversarial coverage: every quote in a warm piece is attributed to the subject, so every quote is evidence, and the subject is a real, living person. No paraphrase inside quotation marks; no "they have said since" carrying an unsourced sentiment - that's a quote wearing a disguise. A real, documented failure: four untraceable quotes attributed to one real person shipped in a single piece before this rule existed in writing.
2. **Every negation is a factual claim about an absence - verify it or cut it.** "No brand tie-in," "no overhead taken," anything of that shape - both shipped false once, in copy that was confident and rhythmical either way. An absence claim gets checked exactly as hard as a positive one; nothing in a source contradicts an invented absence by existing, which is precisely what makes absence claims the easiest to invent without noticing.
3. **The humor, if there's any, never lands on the subject - including backhandedly.** Not their manner, sincerity, size, struggle, or identity, and never a kicker that imports the very deficit it claims to dismiss. A real, named failure: "tells fans they're neurodivergent - and keeps working anyway" shipped as a closer once and was flagged, correctly, as the worst line in its batch - the joke's whole structure concedes that the condition should have stopped them. **The test: if the subject read the line, would they laugh or flinch?**
4. **No overstatement of a true good deed.** Reaching for a bigger frame than the facts support ("atop a melting iceberg" for a modest, real thing) is this register's version of inventing a settlement figure. The real detail is always enough; if it genuinely isn't, the piece is a short, small item, not a bigger claim.
5. **No moral.** The closer never names the virtue outright ("proof that decency still exists"). Let the reader arrive at the conclusion from the facts; stating it for them is doing their work and it reads as unearned.

## The procedure

**Step 0 - what's on the desk.** The same packet any drafting session needs (verified facts, 2-4 captured verbatim particulars, the sourcing trail) plus, specific to this register: the source-traced quote list (rule 1 makes it load-bearing), and - if the piece is partly fictionalized around a real inspiration - a clear line between which claims are the real person's and which are invented texture. The two must never blur mid-draft.

**Step 1 - the warm sentence.** Before any prose, one sentence with all three parts:

> What did they choose, what would the easy version have been, and which verified detail makes you smile?

- **The choice** keeps the subject the active agent of their own story. A piece that loses this structurally can't drift into making the subject a doorway to something else - naming their choice up front makes that failure mode impossible by construction.
- **The forgone easy version** is where the admiration actually lives - this register's real arithmetic is the gap between what they did and what the standard, more profitable version would have been. It doubles as the honesty check: if the easy version wasn't actually easier, there's no story, only an event.
- **The smiling detail** is where any humor comes from. If nothing verified makes you smile, write it warm without a joke - a warm piece with no joke is fine; a joke with no warmth is not.

Worked example: *she signed away a small percentage of one song's royalties to a reforestation fund; the easy version was a press release and a gala; the actual detail is that the fund needed a formal songwriting credit before its lawyers would let the money move at all.*

**This sentence is a required work product** - it should travel with the draft into review, so the stance can be checked or vetoed without anyone having to diagnose the prose to find it.

**Step 2 - the honesty check.** No true answer to the second part of the sentence -> the piece is a short, small item: two or three sentences, understated, and stop there. These are naturally small, quiet stories; a full-length piece built around a small kindness is this register's version of polished emptiness, and length is usually its first symptom - editorializing expands to fill the space where a real reason to write more should have been.

**Step 3 - write it from inside their logic.** Take the subject's own odd, consistent reasoning completely seriously and narrate from inside it, the way you'd take an adversarial subject's stated logic seriously until it convicts itself - here, until it *vindicates* itself. The reader draws the conclusion both times; admiration stated by the writer is a claim asked on faith, admiration the reader arrives at from a specific detail is evidence they judged themselves.

A working toolkit, held loosely - these are what good material reaches for on its own, not techniques to install while drafting:
- **The telling particular** - one precise, slightly surprising *how*. The strongest device, and usually where any humor already lives.
- **The refusal** - what they chose not to do, the profitable default they turned down. **Load-bearing at most once per piece, and at most once across a batch of several pieces** - it's the single fastest-fingerprinting move in this register (six of seven pieces in one real batch closed on it, which is how the pattern got caught).
- **The world that can't parse it** - deadpan bafflement at the subject from outside, amused rather than indignant. If an "everyone else" line reads as an accusation rather than bemusement, it belongs in adversarial coverage of *them*, not here.
- **The uncommented quote** - real, slightly odd, left to stand alone. Commentary after a warm quote is the writer stepping between the reader and the person.
- **Stated with dignity, not inflated** - state a small thing plainly, at its real scale; the honesty of the scale is itself part of what makes it land, not a deficiency to paper over.
- **The endearing flaw** - a small, real oddity. Apparent flawlessness reads as invented even when it's true; a genuine, minor imperfection is what makes the rest credible, as long as it stays texture and never becomes the story.

**Step 3b - the closer: grant continuation, not resolution.** Name the reader's likely reaction in plain words, then find the one verified fact that answers it - but where an adversarial closer typically *denies relief* (it's still happening, nobody's been held to account), a warm closer *grants continuation*: they're still doing it, unbothered, being themselves. The honest moves here are all continuations: it's still happening, it's working anyway, they'd do it again, the rest of the field still hasn't caught up. When no such fact exists, end on the last real thing and stop - a manufactured closer is worse than none.

**Step 4 - hand off with the warm sentence, the quote-trace list, and the piece itself**, and get a second read before it goes out - `sower-writer:final-read`'s two-pass reader (an armed fact-check first, a reading panel second) runs unchanged on a warm piece; if anything, the fact pass should be *more* armed here, not less, because the subject is real, alive, and being written about by name.

## Named failure modes - open only once a draft exists and reads wrong, or when reviewing someone else's

- **Press-release curdle.** Direct praise, launch-copy cadence, "no catch" energy. Test: would the subject's own PR team publish this unchanged? Its most common surface form is flattering-descriptor vocabulary - *inspiring, heartwarming, remarkable, fascinating* - which is worth naming twice: it's a documented machine-writing tell in general (see `sower-writer:line-editing`), and separately, in this register specifically, a praise adjective is a claim asked on faith while a concrete detail is evidence the reader judges themselves. Cut the adjective, keep the detail.
- **The manufactured target.** An outward jab at "everyone else" with no real villain in the verified record. Test: is the everyone-else sentence amused, or is it indignant? Indignant means it's actually adversarial copy wearing this register's clothes.
- **The vanishing subject.** The piece uses the person as a doorway into a broader essay and they stop being its grammatical subject partway through. Test: are they the subject of most sentences, and of the closer specifically?
- **The backhanded kicker.** Praise that imports the very deficit or doubt it claims to reject.
- **Halo inflation.** The overstated version of a true good deed - rule 4's failure, named.
- **Negation monoculture, at the batch level.** If more than one piece in a set leans on "no X, no Y - just Z" as its closer, that's a fingerprint, not a coincidence, even if each individual piece reads fine alone.
- **Sainthood.** No oddity, no texture anywhere in the piece - reads as invented even when every fact in it is true.
- **Solemnity creep.** Memorial-register gravity leaking into a piece about someone alive and still active. Warm is not hushed.
- **The moral.** A closer that names the virtue outright - the warm-register version of a narrator explaining a joke instead of letting it land.
- **Convergence, watched across a batch, not a single piece.** If several pieces written under this register start sharing the same closer move, the same sentence shape, the same warmth-signaling phrase, the fix is Step 1 - a genuinely different true sentence per piece - never a new rhythm rule bolted onto the existing ones. This register exists precisely because uniform machinery applied to warm material converges fast; watch for it doing that to itself too.

## Mistakes to actively flag

- A warm piece drafted before the Step 1 sentence exists - the three-part sentence is what keeps the subject the active agent; skipping it is how the vanishing-subject failure gets back in.
- An unsourced quote, or a quote softened/sharpened past what the primary source actually says, in a piece about a real living person.
- An absence claim ("no brand deal," "no fee taken") that wasn't independently checked.
- A closer that states the virtue instead of a fact that grants continuation.
- Any joke, however mild, whose target is the subject rather than the gap between them and the standard default.
