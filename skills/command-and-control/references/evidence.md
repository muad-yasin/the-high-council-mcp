# Evidence: command-and-control

Open this before a case from `SKILL.md` is retold or used to justify a decision.

## What the cases are

Every case comes from one project. A single owner ran it with a coordinating session called C&C, worker sessions (builders and helpers) and, part of the time, a second reviewing session called Mini-C&C. The cases were collected in November 2026 by a research session that read the project's standing rules and its dated decisions, the sessions' own notes, and the owner's typed messages, which it found by searching the session logs for phrases. It checked each quotation against its source by script. The quotations are private and are not reproduced here: each case is retold with those role names and without the name of a person, a date, a sum or a share. The few small counts that are left (two helpers, three ways to end a run, twice) describe that one occasion.

The writer of the skill worked from that research and did not check the cases against the private records again.

No outside source was read. Nothing was measured. A case shows that something happened in one setup, and what its owner then decided. It does not show how often such a thing happens elsewhere, or that the rule drawn from it improves a setup.

Some rules record a choice this owner made, not a general law: that the big review sits at the end, that the owner reads chat and not files, that reviews come at the end of a unit, that once a unit is read only lost money or a corrupted record brings a new condition into the release. Another owner may choose otherwise, which is why the skill puts the owner's own written rules above itself.

**W** = the owner's own words on record: a dated decision the project kept, or a message the owner typed. **N** = a session's note of what the owner said or of what happened, not checked against the owner's message. **R** = a rule the setup wrote down for itself and its owner kept.

## Cases that point the other way

The guard in section 2 is about layers a coordinator adds by itself. The same owner asked for checking more than once, and those cases stand beside the others:

- The owner ordered a review of the prompt wording before a paid run, a standing sequence of reviews for the plan of every run, audits before the final review, and one more bounded round of cross-reading (W).
- Earlier in the project the owner accepted extra checking stops by C&C, saying the usage allowed it. Later decisions replaced them with reviews at a unit's end (W).
- The owner turned down caps on effort and a proposal to bring only the hard calls to the second reader, as caution that would cost quality (N).
- The consult before a ruling was itself a duty the owner added (N).

## What moved, and why the skill gives no numbers

Two things changed several times over the weeks the cases cover. How often C&C reviews went back and forth between every step, extra stops and milestones before it settled on whole units at their end. The number of helpers a worker may start went up and down with the job and with the usage left in the week. The skill states the principle each sequence settled on and no count, share or limit: those were true for one plan in one week.

## What no case covers

- When one session is enough, and whether a coordinator is worth having at all. The skill does not say.
- A mechanical guard after a slip of the same kind came back. The cases behind section 2's last bullet show a new rule after one correction. The cases do include slips that came back (a rule lost more than twice, "yes" in the first line twice). Each ends with the owner's word, and none with a guard that a session built. That a repeat earns a guard is `task-scoping`'s rule; that a guard which other sessions would follow goes to the owner is an inference from section 2's first bullet.
- How many sessions one coordinator can oversee.
- A setup with several owners, or one where the workers are people.
- An estimate where no measured run exists. In the case the runs were on disk. That such an estimate goes to the owner named as one is an inference.
- A slip reported in the owner's chat. The written rule says a slip is disclosed at once; that the coordinator's own slip belongs in the owner's chat is an inference from it.
- A session the owner has put to rest. The case behind section 5 is one message to a session with a full context. That a resting session is not woken for an acknowledgement is an inference.
- Four fillings of the message shapes: an owner's word that answers no question, a "done" that was not given, an answer in the owner's own words, and a written rule cited in a ruling. Each is an inference that keeps a shape usable.
- Whether a setup run by these rules produces better work. Nothing here was compared with anything.
