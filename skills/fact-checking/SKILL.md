# Fact-checking

A claim you verified an hour ago and a claim you invented feel identical from the inside, and read identically on the page. Invented claims in real writing are rarely lies: they are the stronger ending reached for, the absence nobody checked, the figure inherited from an earlier draft. This skill finds them before anyone reads the piece, and draws the line on what may be said about real people and organizations.

**This skill owns:** the claim audit of a piece of writing, the rules on quotes and real names, the fact line, and the legal risk check. Source grading in general - tiers, syndicated copies, documented vs inferred, dated claims - belongs to `research-and-sourcing`; this skill applies it to prose and adds what writing needs. **It runs before `drafting`, not after**: facts fitted to finished prose is the usual route by which an invented claim ships. `final-read` re-runs these checks independently before sign-off.

## Hard rules

1. **Never invent a quote for a real person, company, official or spokesperson.** A quotation mark in a piece about real events claims that somebody said those words. A real quote may be trimmed or condensed if the meaning survives; sharpening a hedge into an admission is inventing with extra steps. **An unnamed role binds just as hard** - "a spokesperson said" is still a claim that someone said it. With no usable real quote, the narrator says the strong thing in its own voice; that is usually the better instrument anyway. One published piece carried an invented quote from a thinly renamed executive in the most-read item of its product - and when asked, a model confirmed the quote was invented and called it acceptable by pointing at the product's purely fictional flavour text. The rule had a carve-out; **a rule with a carve-out gets read as the carve-out.**
2. **The fiction exemption is a test, not a list.** (a) Is there a real event behind this piece? If not, invent freely, quotes included. If so, rule 1 binds in full, and **a parody name does not turn a real story into fiction.** (b) If the story is invented, who does the invention land on? Inventing a scandal for a thin parody of a real company is rule 1 arriving by another door. Don't keep a list of "the fictional pieces": one such list was found incomplete within weeks.
3. **In satire or fiction built on real events, alter every real name - on every side of the story.** Alter harder than feels necessary, and check the whole phrase for a real trademark surviving inside the parody (one parody brand still contained the unaltered name of the company it riffed on). Name checks tend to run on villains only, so the one unaltered name ships on the *good* side - a real plaintiff, a charity, a victim. Genericize it ("a civil-rights group") and keep the real name in the sourcing note. Two entities that separately did the same thing get two parodies, never one merged one. (Straight reporting names people accurately - and then the name is itself a claim to check.)
4. **The fact line.** Verified events, figures, rulings, orderings and timelines are frozen. Never add a figure, an allegation, a causal link or a second incident for effect. Yours: interpretation, analogy, arithmetic on verified numbers (show it in the note), rounding a verified figure, and small staged beats that change how a scene *plays*, never what a reader believes *happened*. Unsure which side something is on? Treat it as a fact. **Audit the last sentence hardest** - reaching for a stronger ending is the single most reliable way to invent a fact.
5. **Four kinds of claim go to the primary document: figures, rulings, sentences, superlatives.** An amount, a court or regulator's finding (including *charged* vs *settled* vs *found liable* vs *convicted*), a prison term, any "first / largest / only". Syndicated copies of one wire story are one source (`research-and-sourcing`). One batch of five stories carried three false claims inherited from an earlier pass - a penalty figure that traced nowhere, a spend figure off by roughly 2.8x, a second conviction described as the same offence when the record said perjury. Each died on contact with the primary record; none necessarily dies against two agreeing articles.
6. **A rewrite gets the same verification as a first draft.** The existing published text is not a source - it demonstrably contains claims nobody checked - and an old sourcing note is a lead, not a citation. An inherited false claim ships exactly as badly as an invented one.
7. **Replace a failed claim with something true; never blur it into vagueness.** Vagueness is how a failed claim survives. In the recorded cases the true replacement - a real regulatory case for a wrong pay-cut claim, the real current price gap for a wrong price-hike claim - was stronger than the invention.

## Procedure

1. **List every load-bearing claim across every layer** - headline, subhead, body, captions, alt text: events, figures, dates, orderings, quotes, causal links, superlatives, and absences ("no fee", "no strings").
2. **Trace or search each one.** Verdict per claim: `traced` / `re-verified` / `FLAGGED (searched: <terms>)`. Anything that traces nowhere is FLAGGED - never the benefit of the doubt. One flagged load-bearing claim sends the piece back to research before anyone reads it for quality.
3. **Capture two to four verbatim particulars before drafting** - a number at the source's own odd precision, a documentary detail (a page number, a dimension, a product name), a real quote in its real wording, and especially **a paired number**: two official figures for the same thing that disagree. A summary of a source can only produce a summary of a story. Particulars are evidence, not diction: put them in your own words, except a real quote presented as a quote.
4. **Write the one-line read:** what the strongest particular proves and who should care - or an honest "no read, routine", which tells the writer to keep it short.
5. **Grade documented vs inferred, and never wire a consequence to an inference.** Where a piece drives something downstream - a rating, a recommendation, a price, a call to action - only documented claims may carry it. Saying the limit of the evidence in the copy ("nobody can prove the timing was deliberate; you can read the calendar") reads as more credible, not less.
6. **Hand over the note**: verified claims in plain prose with every source as an inline link, the particulars, the read, documented vs inferred, every quote and where it came from, and any disagreement between sources stated rather than silently resolved. Never a bare "verified" - that is a claim about work done, not evidence of it. Never backfill a note from memory.

## Mistakes to flag

- A quotation mark on a real-sourced piece that traces nowhere; "a spokesperson said" treated as safer than a named quote.
- The absolute - "every time", "nobody ever" - where the record describes specific failures. The cheapest tell to check.
- The time served given as if it were the sentence; the timeline inverted ("two weeks after" when it was six months before).
- A synonym stated as the record's own word - "the word used was generosity" when the documented word was "philanthropy".
- A search summary standing in for the document; an absence claim nobody checked.
- "Experts say" / "critics argue" with nobody named.
- An indictment written as a conviction.
- An unaltered real name in satire, on either side of the story.
- A consequence (rating, price, call to action) resting on an inference.
- A failed claim softened instead of replaced.
