# Verification - method, grading, and where claims break

## The method, claim by claim

1. **List every load-bearing claim across every layer.** Events, figures, dates, orderings, quotes presented as real, causal links, superlatives, absences.
2. **Trace it or search it** - never "does this sound right". The default for anything that traces nowhere is `FLAGGED`, with the search terms recorded.
3. **One verdict per claim:** `traced` / `re-verified` / `FLAGGED (searched: <terms>)`.
4. **One flagged load-bearing claim sends the piece back** before anyone reads it for quality. There is no point admiring an ending that is about to be deleted for being false - and the ending is where the falsehood most often lives.

**Open the search every time.** The confident feeling attaches equally to a claim checked an hour ago and one invented an hour ago; "yes, I checked that" is exactly the operation that fails.

## Grading, applied to a piece of writing

`research-and-sourcing` owns the general tiers. For published writing, three buckets do most of the work:

- **A specific documented event** - a named case, a settlement, a regulator's finding, a court record, a filing. Safe to state as fact.
- **A real recurring pattern** - true in aggregate, not a citable fact about *this* actor. Usable only if the piece is honest that it is a pattern; first check whether a specific event exists for this exact subject.
- **Nothing.** Not usable. Replace it with something true rather than blurring it.

**Figures, rulings, sentences and superlatives go to the primary document** - the ruling, the filing, the regulator's own release - not an article about it. Syndicated copies of one wire story count once; "two independent sources" is a quota, and a quota can be met without meeting its purpose. Read the count as a floor under the grading, never as the test.

### When the source itself is the question

If you don't know whether a site or outlet is trustworthy, **read laterally**: leave the page and see what other sources say about it, instead of reading further down the page you are on. In a study of professional fact-checkers, historians and students, the fact-checkers read laterally and the others mostly didn't (Wineburg and McGrew, "Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise", *Teachers College Record* 121(11), 2019, [doi:10.1177/016146811912101102](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102)). Mike Caulfield's [SIFT moves](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/) package it: Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims, quotes and media back to the original context. The last move is the primary-document rule above.

## Capturing particulars

Before drafting, two to four verbatim concrete items per piece:

- **A number with odd precision**, at the source's own precision.
- **A physical or documentary detail** - a page number, a dimension, a product name, a phrase written somewhere.
- **A real quote**, in its real wording.
- **A paired number** - two official figures for the same thing that disagree: a no-bid price against a competitive bid, one filing against another. Hunt for these; no comment is needed.

**Why this is the highest-value part of research:** recording only *what happened* leaves the writer nothing but a restatement to make, and the prose defaults to generality. The best-received lines on one content project were exactly this kind of detail - a clause located on a specific page of a long terms-of-service document, an area expressed in an absurdly small fraction of a football field - and both arrived by accident. Capturing them deliberately is the point.

**Particulars are evidence, never diction.** Institutional phrasing pasted intact reads as machine output even when true - a verbatim "white recreational vehicle" lifted from real reporting drew an immediate rejection. Only a real quote presented as a quote keeps source wording.

## Documented vs inferred

Grade each claim **documented** (a filing, a ruling, or one of the four confirmed against the primary document) or **inferred** (a pattern, a coincidence of timing, a reasonable read of motive).

- **Never wire a consequence to an inference.** A rating, a recommendation, a price or a call to action rests on documented claims only.
- **Stating the limit of the evidence is strength, not weakness.** "Nobody can prove the timing was deliberate; what you can do is read the calendar" is more credible than asserting the link, and keeps the claim on the opinion side of the line `legal-guardrail.md` turns on.
- **Vague attribution is a sourcing hole first.** "Experts say", "critics argue", "observers note" trace to nothing, and a claim that failed verification tends to hide there.

## Where claims actually break - hunt hardest here

Each of these shipped, or nearly shipped, in real work.

- **The stronger ending.** Audit the last sentence first and hardest.
- **The absolute.** "Every time", "nobody ever", "not a single". Invention arrives as an absolute where the record describes specific failures.
- **The summary standing in for the document.** A search result's summary of a filing reads complete, which is what makes it dangerous. In one case, opening the regulator's own release surfaced two findings the summary never mentioned - both stronger than the invented line they replaced.
- **The inherited claim.** On a rewrite, the existing text reads like a source and isn't one. The claims you didn't write are the ones nobody else will question either.
- **Sentence versus time served.** Two different numbers, both reported; the gap is often the story. Caught twice in four batches on unrelated stories.
- **The timeline inversion.** Orderings fail quietly on a single source; the second source is where they surface.
- **The synonym stated as fact.** A word attributed to the record that the record doesn't contain. The real word is usually sharper.
- **The invented absence.** "No overhead", "no brand tie-in". Nothing in a source contradicts an absence by existing.
- **The "both sides" fact.** Even-handedness about a conflict is a stance, never a claim about who a vendor sold to.
- **The unaltered real name** in satire, anywhere in the copy, including on the good side.

## The note that travels with the piece

One honest paragraph, kept where the piece lives:

- what is documented and what is inferred;
- **the verification URLs themselves**, not a summary of them;
- every quote - which are real, which are condensed, and from where;
- any disagreement between sources, stated rather than silently resolved;
- and, if the piece is fiction under the two-question test, that it is - explicitly.

**List the quotes specifically.** A note that lists invented texture but omits the quotes reads as exhaustive when it isn't - that exact gap let an invented quote sit in a most-read piece. **Never backfill a note from memory;** an honest URL can't be reconstructed, so unrecorded research gets redone.
