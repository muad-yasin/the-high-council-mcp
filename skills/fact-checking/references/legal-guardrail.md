# Legal guardrail - real people and organizations

> **General information, not legal advice.** Nothing here is a legal opinion or a substitute for a lawyer. It exists so you can recognise the shape of a risk while writing, when it is cheap to avoid. Anything commercial that satirises identifiable real people or companies should get a media/IP lawyer's review before launch. Law differs by country; the two frameworks below (Germany/EU and the US) genuinely disagree in places. Sources were checked against primary text on 2026-09-26.

The working rules are in `../SKILL.md` (hard rules 1-4). This file is the background - so you can tell a rule with a court behind it from a rule that is only caution - plus two rules that only make sense against it, and a checklist.

## The distinction that matters most: fact vs opinion

Both frameworks hinge on it. German law separates a **factual assertion** (*Tatsachenbehauptung*), which can be proven true or false, from an **expression of opinion** (*Meinungsäußerung*), which cannot. Opinion is broadly protected; a false or unprovable factual assertion is not. US defamation law likewise requires a false statement *of fact*.

**Every hard rule in the skill is a way of staying on the opinion side of that line.** A fabricated quote, an invented settlement figure, an invented second incident: all factual assertions. A forceful narrator verdict about documented conduct is opinion built on fact - the strongest ground satire has.

**Why an invented quote is the sharpest exposure:** it turns your opinion into someone else's statement of fact, and it survives every other defence. A parody name doesn't help if the referent is recognisable; truth doesn't help because the words were never said. On *altered* real quotes, the US Supreme Court held in [*Masson v. New Yorker Magazine*, 501 U.S. 496 (1991)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/501/496) that a deliberate alteration does not by itself show knowledge of falsity "unless the alteration results in a material change in the meaning conveyed by the statement." That is close to the "trim, never change the meaning" rule. It offers nothing for a quote that was never said.

## Two rules that follow from the law

- **Grounding is a defence - don't throw it away.** When a piece satirises a parody of a real company, its footing is that the bad thing is actually true of the real one. The parody name addresses trademark exposure; the sourcing addresses everything else. Inventing conduct for a parody of a real company discards the second and buys nothing. One content project closed this path outright: a satire target traceable to a real company is grounded in something that company actually did, or the piece isn't written. Where a piece was already invented, it was rewritten around real material rather than deleted.
- **Sweep names from the category, not from the examples.** A name rule written for companies misses agencies, regulators, NGOs, cities, buildings, awards and nationality adjectives, because nobody put them in the search. One project swept country names, stopped at borders, and found real city names in shipped copy months later. Genuinely generic terms nobody owns ("federal investigators", statute names, public holidays) stay as they are. A deliberate exception - a real name kept because recognition is the point - is fine if it is written down where the piece lives, with its reason.

## Germany and the EU

- **Expression, art and their limits.** [Art. 5 Grundgesetz](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/gg/art_5.html) protects expression (para. 1) and art (para. 3); para. 2 limits expression by general laws, youth protection and "the right to personal honour". Civil courts also protect the general personality right derived from Arts. 1 and 2.
- **Satire is read in two layers.** The Federal Constitutional Court separates a satire's statement core (*Aussagekern*) from its exaggerated packaging (*Einkleidung*) and judges the packaging less strictly than the core ([BVerfGE 75, 369, 1987](https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv075369.html); [BVerfGE 86, 1, 1992](https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv086001.html)). In practice: exaggeration is licence; a false factual core is not.
- **Criminal honour offences:** [§ 185 StGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__185.html) insult; [§ 186](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__186.html) defamation by a factual assertion "unless this fact is provably true"; [§ 187](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__187.html) knowingly false defamation. Note § 186's structure: the risk attaches to an *unprovable* assertion, not only a false one - which is why the primary-document rule matters even more for German publishing.
- **Balancing.** Courts weigh expression against personality rights, allowing more latitude for commentary on public figures and matters of public debate than for attacks on private individuals. The same joke about a powerful executive and about a private citizen are different legal objects.
- **Abusive criticism (*Schmähkritik*)** is the outer edge: speech that has lost any recognisable connection to a matter and only degrades the person. The Constitutional Court construes it narrowly and requires courts to justify the finding ([four decisions of 19 May 2020, press release](https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Pressemitteilungen/DE/2020/bvg20-049.html)). The reference case in public memory is the 2016 "Schmähgedicht" about the Turkish president: Hamburg courts enjoined most of the poem (LG Hamburg 2017, OLG Hamburg 2018), the Federal Court of Justice refused leave to appeal in 2019, and the Constitutional Court declined the complaint in 2022 without reasons. The criminal offence at the centre of that affair, § 103 StGB (insulting foreign heads of state), was repealed with effect from 1 January 2018 ([BGBl. I 2017 S. 2439](https://dejure.org/BGBl/2017/BGBl._I_S._2439)).
- **Reporting on suspicion.** The German Press Council's [Press Code](https://www.presserat.de/pressekodex.html), section 13, applies the presumption of innocence to the press; guideline 13.1 requires the language of reporting to distinguish clearly between suspicion and proven guilt. **An indictment is not a conviction**, and a piece that treats one as the other has made a false factual assertion no matter how funny it is. Write the status the record states - charged, settled, found liable, convicted.

## United States

- **Defamation** requires a false statement of fact, published, causing harm, with fault; public figures must also show actual malice (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard). Opinion and parody that no reasonable reader takes as fact are generally not actionable.
- **Right of publicity** is state law and varies. Video-game cases are the closest analogue to in-product fiction, and both well-known ones failed: *Lohan v. Take-Two Interactive*, [31 N.Y.3d 111 (2018)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ny-court-of-appeals/1892879.html) - the court accepted that a game avatar *can* be a "portrait" under New York law but found the character not recognisable as her; and a Los Angeles court dismissed Manuel Noriega's suit over a game character in 2014 under California's anti-SLAPP statute (secondary reports; no primary document located). The practical advice survives both: when in doubt, fictionalize.
- **Trademark and parody.** In [*Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products*, 599 U.S. 140 (2023)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-148), a unanimous Court held that the *Rogers v. Grimaldi* test - long read as shielding expressive works - does not apply when a mark is used "as a designation of source for the infringer's own goods", and that the noncommercial-use exclusion from dilution does not shield parody used that way. **The line to remember:** a parody name used as a character or company *inside* your work is expressive use, the stronger case. The same name used as *your product's own branding* - the icon, the box, the store page, merchandise - is the weak case. Keep parodies inside the fiction.
- **Copyright** protects expression, not ideas. A general trope is free; a specific plot lifted and renamed edges toward a derivative work - a different, higher-stakes category than satirising real facts about public actors. Clearance for one is not clearance for the other.
- **Disclaimers are not a shield on their own.** "Any resemblance is coincidental" doesn't help where the resemblance is obviously not coincidental. What helps is that each statement is either true or not a statement of fact.

## Checklist before anything with a real referent publishes

1. Every quotation mark traces to a real utterance - or the piece is fiction under the two-question test.
2. No invented conduct attributed to a parody of a real entity.
3. In satire, every real name altered, on every side of the story, checked across the whole phrase.
4. The category sweep run, not just the examples that prompted the rule.
5. Charged / settled / found liable / convicted stated as the record states it.
6. Every figure, ruling, sentence and superlative traced to a primary document.
7. Anything inferred marked as inferred - in the text where it matters, in the note always.
8. No parody mark used as your own product's branding.
9. Deliberate exceptions written down where the piece lives, with their reason.
10. A lawyer has seen it before it earns money.
